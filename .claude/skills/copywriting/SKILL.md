# Copywriting voice — DigitalMikaelson

DigitalMikaelson sells a low-cost ebook ("Gagne ta vie sans diplôme") through a
WhatsApp-centric funnel with paid ads, to young francophone Africans in RDC
dealing with unemployment or underpaid work. The brand's whole pitch is that
the reader already has something valuable (a skill) and just needs a clear,
honest path to turn it into income. The copy has to sound like that promise,
not undercut it.

## Language and register

Write in plain, everyday French, the register someone would actually use
talking to a younger sibling or a friend, not corporate or academic French.
No jargon. If a simpler word exists, use it.

**Tu vs vous** is a deliberate choice, not a fixed rule:
- Default to **tu** for anything one-to-one and intimate: the ebook itself,
  WhatsApp messages, anything that reads like a personal conversation with
  one reader.
- Use **vous** when the piece explicitly addresses a broad or mixed
  collective audience at once (e.g. an ad opening with "que vous soyez
  salarié, étudiant, ou sans emploi..."). This is intentional for ads that
  need to speak to several audience segments in the same breath.
- Never mix tu and vous within the same piece. If you're extending or
  editing a piece and notice it drifts between registers, don't silently
  pick one, flag it to the user and ask which register that specific piece
  should keep.

## Tone: encouraging, not preachy

The brand's job is to sound like someone who understands the reader's
situation and wants to help, not like someone listing rules at them. That
distinction should shape actual sentence construction, not just vocabulary:

- Avoid repeating an analytical scaffold like "le problème c'est que X...
  le problème c'est que Y..." twice in the same piece. Said once it's fine;
  said twice it starts to read like a lesson being taught.
- Prefer warm, connective phrasing that acknowledges the reader before it
  explains anything: "je sais ce que c'est de...", "ça ne devrait pas
  rester pour vous/toi seul(e)", rather than flat declarative statements
  of fact.
- The brand can name the reader's situation (job search not working, pay
  too low, studies with no clear outcome) but shouldn't dwell on it or
  dramatize it. One sentence of recognition, then move toward the
  possibility, not a paragraph of pain.
- CTAs read as an invitation, not a command. Don't end on a bare imperative
  alone ("Écrivez-moi maintenant."). Close on something that signals
  ongoing help: "écrivez-moi sur WhatsApp, je vous accompagne" rather than
  just "écrivez-moi sur WhatsApp."

## Signature brand elements

Reuse these for consistency instead of inventing new equivalents:

- **"Pas de blabla, juste ce qu'il faut faire, concrètement."** (or a close
  variant) is the brand's standard way of promising no-fluff, practical
  content. Reach for this pattern when a piece needs to promise simplicity.
- **The VENDRE framework**: Visibilité, Engagement, Notoriété, Distribution,
  Relance, Évolution. This is established brand IP, use the real letters
  and order if the framework comes up.
- **Oncle David**: a real family anecdote (an uncle, a menuisier
  [carpenter] who became a digital-product creator without an ad budget).
  Use him as a first-person illustrative anecdote when the copy needs a
  concrete human example, not a generic third-party case study.
- **Illustrative skill domains**: when you need examples of "a skill
  someone already has," draw from this established set rather than
  inventing new ones: cuisine, couture, langues, menuiserie, coiffure,
  comptabilité.

## Never fabricate

This is the hardest constraint and the most important one. Never invent:
- Statistics, percentages, or study citations
- Testimonials, customer names, or specific results ("Marie a gagné 200$")
- Platform names, tool names, or payment method availability not confirmed
  by the user or verified research
- Personal biographical details not given by the user
- **Prices**: never state a price unless the user has explicitly told you
  to include one in that specific piece. Different pieces in the funnel
  intentionally omit or include price depending on where the reader is
  (e.g. the pre-purchase summary video omits it, the shop-link message
  states it).

If a piece of copy would be stronger with a specific fact and none exists
yet, either ask the user for it or write the generic/illustrative version.
A vague-but-honest sentence beats a specific-but-invented one.

## Run every draft through the humanizer pass

Before treating a piece as final, check it against the
`anthropic-skills:humanizer` patterns (invoke that skill directly for
anything long or high-stakes): no em dashes or en dashes, no rule-of-three
padding, no inflated-significance language ("marque un tournant",
"témoigne de..."), no AI-vocabulary words (crucial, delve, tapestry,
testament, souligne...), varied sentence length, concrete detail over
vague abstraction. A script full of uniform, tidy sentences reads as
machine-written even if the content is right.

## Timing video and audio scripts

French voiceover runs at roughly **2.5–2.8 spoken words per second**
including natural pauses. Before calling a script final for a target
duration, count the words and check the math:

- 25s script → roughly 65–90 words comfortable
- 55s script → roughly 140–155 words comfortable

If a draft overshoots, say so explicitly and either trim it or propose
extending the duration. Don't silently let a script run long, the person
producing the video needs to know before they shoot it.

## Flag, don't silently fix

If you notice a genuine inconsistency while writing or editing (a chapter
count that doesn't match a cross-reference, a register that drifts, a
promise made in one asset that another asset contradicts), call it out to
the user explicitly rather than quietly picking a resolution. These pieces
form a funnel; small mismatches between assets are the kind of thing that
looks sloppy once a reader hits two of them in a row.
